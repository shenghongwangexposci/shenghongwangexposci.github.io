PAMPA mass balance

Simulates where a chemical goes during a parallel artificial membrane permeability assay (PAMPA) — donor well, lecithin/dodecane membrane, acceptor well, headspace, and the plastic plate — and the effective permeability (Pe) the assay would report. Based on the mechanistic mass balance model in Wang, Zhang, Li, Illa & Li, Environment International 170 (2022) 107589.

Chemical properties

Assay conditions

The UWL thickness is set by stirring: about 0.2 cm in an unstirred plate, falling to roughly 25–100 µm with individual-well stirring at 100–600 rpm. Thinner layers raise Pe, most strongly for hydrophobic chemicals.

Method

Five compartments — donor, membrane, acceptor, headspace, and the plastic wall — exchange the chemical by diffusion across stagnant boundary layers (Fick’s first law), giving a linear system of five differential equations that is solved exactly. The membrane is treated as a single boundary layer split into two halves facing the donor and acceptor. Effective permeability is then computed from the modeled donor and acceptor concentrations exactly as a laboratory PAMPA would (Eq. 1 of the paper, VD = VA):
Pe = −2.303·VAVD / [(VA+VD)·A·(t − τSS)] · log10[(CD − CA)/(CD + CA)].

Partitioning: membrane–water K = (0.05·DOW)0.6; plastic–water KPW = 0.06·KOW; air–water DAW = fn·KAW, with KAW = KOW/KOA and fn the neutral fraction at the assay pH. Diffusivities in water, the membrane, plastic and air are estimated from molar mass and McGowan volume. Fixed plate geometry: VD = VA = 0.2 mL, membrane area 0.3 cm², membrane thickness 125 µm.

Note: Eq. 1 assumes steady-state transport and requires the donor and acceptor not to have equilibrated. For hydrophobic chemicals the time to steady state can exceed the incubation time, and with thin unstirred water layers or long incubations the two wells can equilibrate — then Pe cannot be calculated. The lag time τSS is fixed at 19 min, the generic value used in the published model; it suits hydrophilic chemicals but underestimates the lag for hydrophobic ones (paper §3.3.3).

Note: the unstirred water layer (UWL) thickness is controlled by stirring. A thicker layer lowers Pe, most strongly for hydrophobic chemicals, whose measured Pe may mostly reflect the UWL rather than the membrane itself.

Stirring speed option: not part of the original model. Stirring speed f (rpm) is converted to UWL thickness with the power law fitted to individual-well-stirred PAMPA data by Avdeef et al. (2004, Eur. J. Pharm. Sci. 22, 365–374), as reported in Avdeef (2022, ADMET & DMPK 10(4)): PUWL = 23.1×10−6·f0.709 cm/s and δW = DW/PUWL, where DW is the chemical’s water diffusivity estimated by the model, capped at 0.2 cm (unstirred). The resulting thickness is applied to each side of the membrane, as the model does for an entered thickness. The fit was made over roughly 50–600 rpm with magnetic stirring; treat values outside that range, or for orbital shakers, as rough.

Example inputs: log KOW and pKa as given for the case-study chemicals in Fig. 5 of the paper; molar mass and McGowan volume calculated from molecular formulas; KOA derived from Henry’s law constants in EPA’s httk database (OPERA predictions). Treat results as screening-level.
